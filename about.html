The Company

Fellowship Law was built from a shared belief that legal advocacy should remain deeply human.

The firm’s founders arrived at that belief from different directions.

After years of practice in complex plaintiff-side litigation, co-founder Mary Olszewska began envisioning a different kind of law firm — one less shaped by the hierarchies and transactional pressures that often define modern professional life, and more grounded in fellowship, moral independence, and genuine human connection.

Over time, she found herself increasingly drawn toward a model of advocacy that recognized the human realities on both sides of the attorney-client relationship. Too often, modern professional culture asks people to perform care rather than truly embody it — reducing clients to matters and inventories and lawyers to measures of productivity, while losing sight of the emotional weight carried by both.

She believed sophisticated advocacy did not require sacrificing empathy, and that meaningful legal work begins by remembering the person at the center of the case. Clients deserve lawyers willing to listen closely, think independently, and remain grounded in the human realities beneath the legal conflict itself.

At the same time, co-founder Zachary Weil was experiencing a profound shift within the film industry after years of success, including Emmy award-winning documentary films and projects recognized at major international festivals. His work as a filmmaker had always centered on story, memory, and institutional accountability — themes that would eventually become foundational to Fellowship Law Group itself.

Together, those experiences shaped a different vision for what a litigation practice could be. Thoughtful. Trial-ready. Collaborative. Grounded in the belief that institutions must never lose sight of the human beings inside them.

Not louder.
Not more performative.
Not built around intimidation.

But rooted in careful advocacy, moral independence, and genuine human connection.

Many clients come to Fellowship Law during periods of extraordinary uncertainty. Some are confronting unlawful treatment at work. Some are survivors seeking accountability after years of silence. Others are artists, professionals, and creators trying to navigate industries and agreements that were never designed with equal footing in mind — spaces where experience, leverage, and institutional knowledge can be unevenly distributed.

Often, clients arrive feeling isolated, overwhelmed, or powerless.

Our work begins by helping clients regain orientation.

This means listening carefully. Investigating thoroughly. Preparing relentlessly. It means uncovering facts, translating lived experience into language institutions must confront, and helping clients reclaim authorship over their own story. We believe sophisticated advocacy and human compassion are not opposing ideas. The strongest representation often requires both.

The name Fellowship reflects the spirit of the firm itself: the belief that difficult passages are best navigated together, and that meaningful legal work begins not with ego, but with trust, steadiness, and shared purpose.

Mary Olszewska

Partner

Mary Olszewska is a plaintiffs’ trial lawyer who represents people in some of the most difficult and consequential moments of their lives: employees confronting unlawful treatment at work, survivors seeking accountability, and clients whose experiences have been minimized, distorted, or denied by more powerful institutions.

Her work is grounded in a simple but demanding belief: legal power is a privilege.

Litigation gives lawyers access to rules, judges, juries, and procedures that can restore balance where imbalance has taken root. Mary treats that power with seriousness — not as a tool for ego, but as a responsibility held in service of clients.

Mary’s career has taken her through complex plaintiff-side litigation, employment law, sexual harassment and assault cases, class actions, commercial disputes, and sexual abuse litigation. She has represented clients in high-stakes matters involving powerful defendants and institutions, helped secure significant recoveries for clients, and participated in trial work involving deeply consequential abuse claims.

But the center of Mary’s practice has always been human.

She is drawn to cases where someone’s experience has been minimized, distorted, or denied. Survivors of abuse. Employees retaliated against or pushed out. People forced to carry the consequences of someone else’s misconduct while institutions protect themselves.

Mary understands that difficult cases require more than aggression. They require steadiness. They require the ability to look directly at painful facts, hold them carefully, and transform them into clear legal advocacy. Mary does not treat clients’ suffering as something shameful or untouchable. She treats it as something that can be understood, organized, and brought into the light through the disciplined process of law.

To Mary, trial work is not simply combat. A jury trial is a civic ritual — community members gathered to resolve a real dispute between real people. It is emotional, imperfect, raw, and profoundly human. That is part of what makes it meaningful.

Long before Mary entered a courtroom, she was already sensitive to imbalance.

She was raised by an immigrant single mother whose resilience shaped her understanding of strength. Her mother came to the United States in her late twenties, leaving behind her family, language, and familiar world. Soon after, she had to leave an abusive relationship and raise two young daughters while learning English, learning a new trade, and finding her way through a country that did not make the path easy.

Mary watched her mother survive, adapt, and keep going.

She also saw something that would stay with her: women are often the ones holding everything together, yet the world repeatedly treats them as less powerful than they are. That contradiction struck Mary early. It still does.

As a child, Mary was intensely smart, independent, rebellious, and alert to unfairness. She liked to argue, but not simply for the sake of argument. She wanted to know what was true. She was especially sensitive to moments when someone with less power was mocked, dismissed, or cornered.

School and sports gave her structure. They gave her discipline, competition, and a physical place to put her intensity. Later, in college, Mary found the intellectual world that felt most alive to her: classics, philosophy, mythology, and linguistics. She was drawn to language, meaning, story, and the old questions of human nature.

Law school became a natural extension of those interests. The law gave structure to conflict. It had rules. It had judges to enforce them. It offered a way to test competing versions of reality within a system designed, however imperfectly, to reach truth.

At first, Mary thought she might belong in transactional law. But once she entered that world, she realized something essential about herself: she needed the challenge and immediacy of litigation. She needed the human stakes. She needed the place where preparation, intuition, language, and courage all mattered at once.

Over time, Mary’s understanding of success changed. Earlier in life, she believed power was connected to image, title, and institutional identity. Experience taught her something different. Real power is quieter than that. It is patience. A steady voice. The refusal to give up. The willingness to shed what no longer fits and rebuild something more honest.

Fellowship Law grew from that transformation.

Mary wanted to build a firm that did not merely perform care, but embodied it — for clients and for the people doing the work. A firm organized around fellowship rather than hierarchy. A council of caring, intelligent advocates for people navigating systems that often forget their humanity.

Outside the law, Mary is the mother of a young daughter and spends much of her life inside that child’s magical world. She loves forests, nature, ritual, and the quiet spellwork of cooking in the kitchen.

At Fellowship Law, she helps clients move through uncertainty, recover their voice, and confront what must be confronted — with clarity, courage, and care.

Zachary Weil

Partner

Zachary Weil serves as a partner at Fellowship Law Group, where his work is guided by a deep concern for the historical record, the public good, and the ways institutions shape the lives of ordinary people.

Zack’s path to the law has been circuitous, but not accidental. Again and again, his work has returned to the same themes: memory, accountability, creative expression, and the obligation of powerful institutions to tell the truth.

Raised in Miami, Florida, Zack grew up with a love of basketball, music, history, and all things space. Like many children drawn to rockets and exploration, he once imagined becoming an astronaut. His aptitude for math had other plans. But that early fascination with space never left him. Over time, it became part of a broader interest in history, storytelling, and the preservation of human experience.

After graduating from Cornell University with a degree in history, Zack began working in the film industry in New York City. Still searching for the right path, he returned to Miami and attended Florida International University College of Law. After law school, he moved to Los Angeles, where he immersed himself in the independent film world.

What began as one-off creative work gradually became something more durable. Zack built a video production company from the ground up, first helping nonprofit organizations tell their stories with clarity and purpose. He later moved into documentary filmmaking, producing and directing historical films centered on the American space program. His documentaries When We Were Apollo and When We Were Shuttle each earned Regional Emmy® Awards.

Zack also worked in the narrative feature world, producing award-winning projects with Oscar®-nominated actors that appeared at SXSW, the Toronto International Film Festival, and other major festivals and platforms. Through this work, he learned not only how creative projects are made, but how fragile they can become when legal, financial, ownership, rights, and logistical concerns are not handled with care.

This experience now informs his entertainment and intellectual property practice. Zack understands what it feels like to build something from nothing, to protect and nurture a creative vision, and to worry whether the legal foundation underneath the work is strong enough to support it. He advises filmmakers, producers, artists, and creative companies on the legal architecture of their projects, including entity formation, contracts, financing, production counsel, intellectual property protection, music and footage licensing, chain of title, fair use, clearances, insurance, festivals, and distribution.

His goal in this work is practical and protective: to help creative professionals move forward with confidence, without losing sight of the art, the relationships, or the story they are trying to tell.

Zack’s love of history and the public good also shape another central part of his practice. Increasingly, his work focuses on public-interest law/federal agency and administrative litigation, particularly where government action threatens access to knowledge, public accountability, or the preservation of institutional memory.

At Fellowship Law, Zack brings the perspective of both a lawyer and a storyteller.

He is drawn to cases where the record matters, where the truth has been obscured, and where careful advocacy can help restore clarity and accountability. His work reflects a belief that law, at its best, does more than resolve disputes; it is also a creative and disciplined craft: a way to preserve memory, protect voices, and give people language strong enough to stand against power and systems that would rather obscure and forget.

Ronald Weil

Of Counsel

Ronald Weil serves as Of Counsel to Fellowship Law, bringing decades of trial experience, strategic judgment, and perspective to the firm’s work.

Ron is the father of Zachary Weil and father-in-law of Mary Olszewska. But his role at Fellowship is not symbolic. He serves as a trusted strategic sounding board, trial advisor, and steady source of experience when cases require deeper perspective, seasoned judgment, or preparation for the courtroom.

Raised in the suburbs of New York, Ron was a competitive athlete from an early age. He captained both his high school and college soccer teams, and that instinct — to compete with discipline, lead under pressure, and stay steady inside difficult contests — became part of the foundation of his work as a trial lawyer.

After graduating from Oberlin College and the University of Kansas School of Law, Ron began his legal career at a large Miami law firm, where he became a partner and handled complex litigation involving antitrust claims, construction disputes, intellectual property, securities arbitrations, and business disputes for multinational corporations, financial institutions, and international clients.

He later joined and helped form boutique law firms where his practice continued to span high-stakes commercial litigation, civil fraud proceedings, construction litigation, securities matters, business torts, and complex disputes involving powerful institutions.

That breadth of experience gives Ron a rare perspective. He has represented multinational corporations, banks, international entities, government clients, and individuals facing some of the most difficult moments of their lives. He understands how sophisticated parties think, how complex cases move, and how to bring clarity to disputes that can otherwise feel overwhelming.

A turning point in Ron’s career came when one of his corporate clients referred an employee whose young son had been sexually abused at a local church. In 1994, Ron obtained a $6.7 million jury verdict against the church and the clergyman who abused the child — believed at the time to be the largest jury award for a single victim of sexual abuse. The trial was broadcast on Court TV and drew local and national attention.

That case expanded the focus of Ron’s work. In the years that followed, he represented victims of clergy abuse, employees facing discrimination, consumers in class actions, victims of securities fraud, and clients harmed by serious business misconduct.

Across his career, Ron has obtained verdicts and settlements totaling more than $200 million on behalf of clients. His results include a $12.5 million verdict for a sexual abuse survivor, a $37 million verdict for a victim of defamation, and class-action automobile defect recoveries exceeding $100 million.

At Fellowship Law, Ron’s value lies not only in those results, but in the steadiness and perspective he brings to difficult cases. He has tried cases, resolved cases, built firms, represented institutions, represented individuals, and seen how litigation tests everyone involved. He understands that strong advocacy is not only about force. It is about timing, credibility, preparation, leverage, patience, and knowing when to press.

In that role, Ron serves as a counselor to the counselors — a seasoned advocate whose judgment helps strengthen the firm’s work when the stakes are high.

Outside the law, Ron has long supported Big Brothers Big Sisters and has served as a Big Brother. He remains an avid basketball fan, speaks conversational Spanish, and enjoys cooking new and old recipes — many of which his family, by his own account, eats with varying degrees of enthusiasm. He also continues to go nowhere fast on his Peloton bike.

Ron brings to Fellowship Law the perspective of an elder trial lawyer: competitive, clear-eyed, practical, and steady in the contest.
